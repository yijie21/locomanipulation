\title{SelfAim: Learned Self-Models for Aiming Frozen Humanoid Whole-Body Controllers}

\author{Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review. Paper-ID [add your ID here]}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Humanoid whole-body controllers (WBCs) are increasingly reused as frozen motor layers for loco-manipulation: a planner sends a hand target and the controller moves the whole body. The hand, however, lands away from where it was told. Retraining the controller, measuring the hand during the reach or simulating the controller at run time would each help, but each is costly or unavailable. We present SelfAim, which corrects the command of a frozen WBC instead of its weights. SelfAim learns, from the controller's own free-space reaches in simulation, a self-model that predicts where the hand will settle, and inverts it over reachable commands to choose each reach command once. It needs no task data and no hand sensing, so the same recipe applies to controllers with different command interfaces. In SIMPLE grasping, the self-model of the ScaleBFM controller predicts where the hand settles with a median error of 0.4 to 1.1\,cm on three tasks, and aiming with it outperforms direct execution, raising the successful episodes within 45\,s from 83 to 108. We state three hypotheses and the experiments that extend this evaluation to three controllers in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Learned humanoid whole-body controllers now track whole-body references for walking, crouching and reaching~\cite{scalebfm,sonic,heft,amo,gr00twbc}, and many are released as checkpoints. This makes them natural motor layers for loco-manipulation: a higher level decides where the hand should go, whole-body inverse kinematics (IK) turns the target into a reference, and the frozen controller executes it.

The hand, however, does not land where it was told. A tracking controller trades hand accuracy against balance, and how far it misses depends on the body state and on the reach, so the miss changes from one reach to the next. In SIMPLE~\cite{simple} grasping episodes, the hand of the ScaleBFM controller~\cite{scalebfm} settles a median of 4.0\,cm from where it was commanded.
% src: status §1.7 (B60, reviewed): direct-execution error on the same 235 reach segments of the 120 SIMPLE A episodes, median 4.01 cm.

Three obvious remedies exist, and each is expensive for a frozen controller. Retraining or fine-tuning the controller for precision~\cite{humanoidndm} needs its training pipeline and produces a different controller. Correcting the reach with measured end-effector feedback~\cite{hero} needs a measurement of the hand, which a head camera can lose in the last centimeters of a reach. Optimizing each command by simulating the controller and robot in closed loop~\cite{ttco} needs a simulator and many rollouts at run time.

The miss itself points to a cheaper route, because it repeats: in a given body state, a frozen controller sends the same command to nearly the same place, so a model of the controller can predict the miss before the reach and cancel it in the command. We therefore fix the command, not the controller. SelfAim learns, from the controller's own free-space reaches in simulation, a self-model that predicts where the hand will settle for a given body state and command, and inverts it at command time to send the command whose predicted landing is the target (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). Because the model is accurate only on the kind of commands it was trained on, the inversion searches only kinematically reachable commands. Each reach is commanded once from the body state, with no hand sensing during execution, and the controller is a black box that only has to accept commands and report its body state.
% src (insight evidence): status §1.7: on real SIMPLE episodes the re-collected model predicts the settled hand within 0.36-1.07 cm (three tasks) where direct execution misses by 4.01 cm, i.e. the miss is predictable from state and command. (LML evidence r = 0.85-0.89 removed with LML, 11-26.)

Learning control through a learned forward model goes back to the distal teacher~\cite{jordan1992}. Closest to us in mechanism, OSRAM learns a closed-loop command--response model of a bipedal robot and its own policy and steers low-dimensional commands against it, measuring the response at run time~\cite{osram}. SelfAim instead aims through the whole-body reference interfaces of three released humanoid controllers, measures nothing about the hand at run time, and is judged by grasp success. Unlike TTCO~\cite{ttco}, it moves the simulation offline: the data are collected once per controller, and every later reach costs a few model evaluations and IK re-plans.
% src: novelty report 2026-11-26-wbc-selfmodel-inversion.md (OSRAM full text: Tron1 biped, own policy, low-dim commands l.513-524, mocap response at run time l.330-331; TTCO closed-loop simulation sampling l.215-232).

We build SelfAim for a Unitree G1 with three frozen controllers whose command interfaces differ, ScaleBFM~\cite{scalebfm}, SONIC~\cite{sonic} and HEFT~\cite{heft}. In SIMPLE, a model collected from ScaleBFM's free-space reaches predicts where the hand settles on real grasping episodes, none of which it saw in training, with a median error of 0.36 to 1.07\,cm on three of four tasks. Aiming with it removes a sideways offset of about 6\,cm with which the hand entered the grasp, and SelfAim outperforms direct execution in grasp success, raising ScaleBFM's successful episodes within 45\,s from 83 to 108 of 120. We organize the evaluation around three hypotheses on aim, success and deployment (Sec.~\ref{sec:hyp}) and specify the experiments that extend it to all three controllers in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena~\cite{humanoidarena} (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}). Aiming a frozen controller thus costs one data collection per controller and leaves its weights untouched.

\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================
% src: novelty report ~/research-notebook/novelty/2026-11-26-wbc-selfmodel-inversion.md (grade B) + independent review (B); key quotes spot-checked (TTCO l.215-232, 584-588; OSRAM l.278-283, 513-524; Li l.77-81; EgoHumanoid-V2 l.265-290; HERO l.338-347)
\textbf{Acting through a learned model of oneself.} Goal babbling learns inverse kinematics from a robot's own movements~\cite{rolf2010,saggriac}, and self-modeling robots learn models of their own bodies~\cite{bongard2006}. Other work changes only the input of a fixed controller: neural add-on blocks adapt the reference of a fixed feedback loop~\cite{zhou2017dnn}, learned motion models of a black-box quadruped controller drive footstep planning~\cite{taouil2023}, OSRAM optimizes the references of a deployed policy against a learned command--response model~\cite{osram}, and command adapters for frozen locomotion policies have been audited in simulation~\cite{li2026audit}. Keeping such optimization inside the model's data is a recognized concern in model-based optimization~\cite{mins,lagrassa2024,osram}.

\textbf{Frozen humanoid controllers.} TTCO optimizes the command of a frozen humanoid controller by sampling closed-loop simulations of the controller and robot~\cite{ttco}, and EgoHumanoid-V2 refines training labels offline with a learned local response model of a frozen tracker and fresh rollouts~\cite{egohumanoidv2}. Other work scores candidate motions by their executability under a frozen tracker~\cite{s3,texedo}\TODO{cite SAW, arXiv 2409.10308, after reading it}, adds reference or action residuals for compliance and terrain~\cite{ila,graft}, or learns a tracker's dynamics to fine-tune it~\cite{humanoidndm}. For humanoid grasping, HERO corrects the commanded grasp with measured end-effector feedback~\cite{hero}. SelfAim leaves the controller's weights untouched, needs neither a simulator nor a hand measurement at run time, and aims each reach once from the body state at the command.

\section{Problem Statement and Hypotheses}\label{sec:hyp}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Setting.} A frozen controller $\pi$ receives, at decision time $t$, a reach command $c$ (a hand target that our whole-body IK turns into a reference for $\pi$) and executes it from body state $s_t$. After the reach settles the right hand is at $p=\Phi_\pi(s_t,c)$; $\Phi_\pi$ is unknown and includes the controller, its command interface and the simulator. Given a target $p^\star$, \emph{direct execution} (\Ad) sends $c=p^\star$. SelfAim learns $f_\theta\approx\Phi_\pi$ and sends
\begin{equation}
c^\star=\arg\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\;\lVert f_\theta(s_t,c)-p^\star\rVert ,
\label{eq:inv}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of commands that are kinematically reachable from $s_t$, because the model is trained only on such commands.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} For each we state what would falsify it and what we learn either way.

\emph{H1 (aim).} Inverting $f_\theta$ brings the settled hand closer to the target than \Ad\ for every controller, with no trials at the test condition. \emph{Falsified if} paired hand errors under SelfAim are not lower than under \Ad\ on a benchmark's tasks. \emph{Either way:} whether a frozen WBC's placement error is a learnable function of state and command.

\emph{H2 (success).} Aiming raises grasp success over \Ad\ for every controller without task data, by an amount that depends on the task. \emph{Falsified if} SelfAim does not raise success over \Ad\ on a controller's pre-registered task set. \emph{Either way:} whether a better-aimed hand is worth building into manipulation stacks on frozen controllers.
% H2 history: 11-26 rewrite, positive form confirmed by the user 11-26.

\emph{H3 (deployment).} Moving SelfAim to a new controller or simulator costs one data collection with the same recipe: a model trained on data from the target setting passes the accuracy gate on real task episodes. \emph{Falsified if} a re-collected model fails the gate on most tasks of the target benchmark. \emph{Either way:} how much a new deployment costs.
% H3 = former H4 (deployment cost; user 11-26). Former H3 (training support, S vs R) removed 11-26 with LML: its only evidence was LML (lmlcmp3); in SIMPLE R_D and S_D are bit-identical (the shrink never triggers, all targets in reach; simplecmp review, 5090 11-26). The reachable restriction stays as a design choice in Method.

\textbf{Robot, controllers and commands.} We use a Unitree G1 with Dex3 hands (29 body joints) and three frozen controllers: ScaleBFM~\cite{scalebfm}, SONIC~\cite{sonic} and HEFT~\cite{heft}. A reach command $c$ consists of a hand target point, a 6-D wrist orientation, a stand/squat flag and a squat pelvis height. At the moment of the command, our whole-body IK plans a reference from the \emph{measured} state, and the controller tracks that reference through its native reference interface (e.g.\ body keypoints for ScaleBFM, an encoder over future reference frames for SONIC).
The hand point is 13\,cm ahead of the wrist; a reach has \emph{settled} once pelvis and both hands move slower than 2\,cm/s for 0.5\,s, with a 3\,s cap.

\textbf{Self-model.} $f_\theta$ maps the state at the command and the command to the settled hand point and wrist orientation, both expressed in the measured pelvis-heading frame at the command time. The state contains three body-state snapshots (0.3, 0.2 and 0.1\,s before the command), the current hand pose, and a summary of the IK reference: its final joint configuration, the change from the current one, the planned pelvis pose and the planned duration. Two auxiliary heads predict fall and settle probabilities. The model is an ensemble of five MLPs ($4\times512$, SiLU) trained on bootstrap resamples. Each member predicts a residual on the command, and its last layer is zero-initialised, so an untrained model reproduces direct execution. The loss is a Huber loss (2\,cm) on position, a squared loss on the 6-D rotation, and cross-entropy on the auxiliary heads; we train with AdamW for 30k steps and select the checkpoint by validation median error.

\textbf{Data.} All data come from the frozen controller's free-space reaches in an empty scene of the target simulator, with no task episodes or scenes. (i)~30k resets with two reaches each. About a third of the episodes start with a walk, half of them with walk displacements drawn from the benchmark's approach region, and the rest start standing or squatting. Every reach is planned from the measured state once the previous one has settled. Targets are sampled inside an IK-probed reachable region, 0.05--1.5\,m high. (ii)~15k resets with four short reaches each, of the kind a grasp makes: the hand settles 5--12\,cm in front of the target and then enters slowly; adding them reduces the entry-phase error by 24--32\%. (iii)~Mid-trajectory samples: states every 0.2\,s along a reach become additional starting points, labeled with the same final landing, which lowers the dataset error by a further 14--26\%. Data are split by reset. Collecting the data takes \TODO{simulation time per controller} and training takes \TODO{GPU time}; the controller itself is never updated.

\textbf{Inversion.} Only the position target is changed; the wrist orientation is commanded as is. Starting at $c_0=p^\star$, the inversion iterates $c_{k+1}=\mathrm{clip}\big(c_k+p^\star-f_\theta(s_t,c_k)\big)$ six times, re-planning the reference at each step, and keeps the candidate minimizing $\lVert f_\theta-p^\star\rVert^2+\lambda\,\sigma^2$, where $\sigma$ is the ensemble spread and $\lambda=1$.\dnote{lambda only in code (lml\_tasks.py:82), not in entries}
Each candidate's IK error is checked, and if it exceeds 2\,cm the candidate is bisected back toward $p^\star$ (six halvings) until the error is within 2\,cm. In grasping, the pre-grasp and the entry segment are each commanded once, with no re-correction after observing the result; the model is used for free-hand reaches before contact.

\textbf{Benchmarks and protocol.} SIMPLE~\cite{simple} provides four grasping tasks, BendPick, TabletopGraspMP, XMovePick and XMoveBendPick, with 30 official episodes each that share 40 distinct layouts. In each episode the robot looks once with its head RGB-D camera, plans a stance and a grasp from that view and grasps; success means the object is lifted, counted within 45\,s and within the official time limit. Every episode is run once per arm with the same perception and grasp choice, and arms are compared paired by episode. The simulation is paused while SelfAim computes its commands (for ScaleBFM a median of 3.3--6.7\,s per episode), and this time is not counted toward the official limits. Besides direct execution (\Ad) and SelfAim (\Rr), two arms follow a collision-free arm route planned with cuRobo~\cite{curobo}, executed directly (Route) or with a model-free correction that aims at the controller's own reference (Route+ref). Tasks on which \Ad\ succeeds in at least 24 of 30 episodes are reported but excluded from the pre-registered decision set, and success is tested with exact sign tests over layouts. A self-model is used directly in a new setting only if its prediction error on real task episodes is at most 1.3 times its own validation error, with a 95\% CI upper bound of at most 1.5. HumanoidArena~\cite{humanoidarena} (Isaac Lab~\cite{isaaclab}) completes the evaluation (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Discussion}
% ===========================================================================
SelfAim divides the work of loco-manipulation on a frozen WBC in two: the controller keeps its balance and gait, and a learned model of where it puts the hand corrects every reach command. In SIMPLE this division already removes a systematic miss of ScaleBFM and raises its grasp success, at the cost of one data collection in that simulator. The model is trustworthy where its data are, which is why the inversion stays inside the reachable set. If E1 and E2 confirm H1 and H2 for all three controllers in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena, aiming becomes a reusable step for any manipulation stack built on released WBC checkpoints.
