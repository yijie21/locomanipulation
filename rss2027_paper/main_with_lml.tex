\title{SelfAim: Learned Self-Models for Aiming Frozen Humanoid Whole-Body Controllers}

\author{Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review. Paper-ID [add your ID here]}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Humanoid whole-body controllers (WBCs) are increasingly reused as frozen motor layers for loco-manipulation: a planner sends a hand target and the controller moves the whole body. The hand, however, lands away from where it was told. Retraining the controller, measuring the hand during the reach or simulating the controller at run time would each help, but each is costly or unavailable. We present SelfAim, which corrects the command of a frozen WBC instead of its weights. SelfAim learns, from the controller's own free-space reaches in simulation, a self-model that predicts where the hand will settle, and inverts it over reachable commands to choose each reach command once. It needs no task data and no hand sensing, so the same recipe applies to controllers with different command interfaces. In LocoManiLab grasping, SelfAim brings the hand closer to the grasp point for three frozen WBCs and outperforms direct execution in grasp success for all three, from 15.4 to 36.9\% for ScaleBFM. A new simulator costs one data collection: re-collected in SIMPLE, the model predicts the settled hand on three grasping tasks with a median error of 0.4 to 1.1\,cm. We state four hypotheses and the experiments that complete the evaluation in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

Learned humanoid whole-body controllers now track whole-body references for walking, crouching and reaching~\cite{scalebfm,sonic,heft,amo,gr00twbc}, and many are released as checkpoints. This makes them natural motor layers for loco-manipulation: a higher level decides where the hand should go, whole-body inverse kinematics (IK) turns the target into a reference, and the frozen controller executes it.

The hand, however, does not land where it was told. A tracking controller trades hand accuracy against balance, and how far it misses depends on the body state and on the reach, so the miss changes from one reach to the next. In our LocoManiLab~\cite{locomanilab} experiments, the hands of three such controllers stray from arm routes planned to be collision free by a median maximum of 6.4 to 14.3\,cm.
% src: status §3 lmlroute (19:54, independently reviewed): max hand deviation from the cuRobo route, median 6.4 / 7.9 / 14.3 cm (ScaleBFM / SONIC / HEFT).

Three obvious remedies exist, and each is expensive for a frozen controller. Retraining or fine-tuning the controller for precision~\cite{humanoidndm} needs its training pipeline and produces a different controller. Correcting the reach with measured end-effector feedback~\cite{hero} needs a measurement of the hand, which a head camera can lose in the last centimeters of a reach. Optimizing each command by simulating the controller and robot in closed loop~\cite{ttco} needs a simulator and many rollouts at run time.

The miss itself points to a cheaper route, because it repeats: in a given body state, a frozen controller sends the same command to nearly the same place, so a model of the controller can predict the miss before the reach and cancel it in the command. We therefore fix the command, not the controller. SelfAim learns, from the controller's own free-space reaches in simulation, a self-model that predicts where the hand will settle for a given body state and command, and inverts it at command time to send the command whose predicted landing is the target (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). Because the model is accurate only on the kind of commands it was trained on, the inversion searches only kinematically reachable commands. Each reach is commanded once from the body state, with no hand sensing during execution, and the controller is a black box that only has to accept commands and report its body state.
% src (insight evidence): status §1.1 / §1.3c (r = 0.85-0.89, correction tracks direct-execution error on contact-free reaches; citable per 5090 20:25); status §1.3 (ScaleBFM model landing error 0.7-1.7 cm on IK-reachable commands).

Learning control through a learned forward model goes back to the distal teacher~\cite{jordan1992}. Closest to us in mechanism, OSRAM learns a closed-loop command--response model of a bipedal robot and its own policy and steers low-dimensional commands against it, measuring the response at run time~\cite{osram}. SelfAim instead aims through the whole-body reference interfaces of three released humanoid controllers, measures nothing about the hand at run time, and is judged by grasp success. Unlike TTCO~\cite{ttco}, it moves the simulation offline: the data are collected once per controller, and every later reach costs a few model evaluations and IK re-plans.
% src: novelty report 2026-11-26-wbc-selfmodel-inversion.md (OSRAM full text: Tron1 biped, own policy, low-dim commands l.513-524, mocap response at run time l.330-331; TTCO closed-loop simulation sampling l.215-232).

We test SelfAim on a Unitree G1 with three frozen controllers whose command interfaces differ, ScaleBFM~\cite{scalebfm}, SONIC~\cite{sonic} and HEFT~\cite{heft}. On the seven LocoManiLab grasping tasks, none of which the model saw in training, SelfAim brings the hand closer to the grasp point for all three controllers and outperforms direct execution in grasp success for all three, more than doubling it for ScaleBFM (15.4 to 36.9\%). Re-collecting the data in SIMPLE~\cite{simple} with the same recipe gives a model that predicts the settled hand on real grasping episodes with a median error of 0.36 to 1.07\,cm on three tasks. We organize the evaluation around four hypotheses on aim, success, training support and deployment (Sec.~\ref{sec:hyp}) and specify the experiments that complete it in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena~\cite{humanoidarena} (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}). Aiming a frozen controller thus costs one data collection per controller and leaves its weights untouched.

\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================
% src: novelty report ~/research-notebook/novelty/2026-11-26-wbc-selfmodel-inversion.md (grade B) + independent review (B); key quotes spot-checked (TTCO l.215-232, 584-588; OSRAM l.278-283, 513-524; Li l.77-81; EgoHumanoid-V2 l.265-290; HERO l.338-347)
\textbf{Acting through a learned model of oneself.} Goal babbling learns inverse kinematics from a robot's own movements~\cite{rolf2010,saggriac}, and self-modeling robots learn models of their own bodies~\cite{bongard2006}. Other work changes only the input of a fixed controller: neural add-on blocks adapt the reference of a fixed feedback loop~\cite{zhou2017dnn}, learned motion models of a black-box quadruped controller drive footstep planning~\cite{taouil2023}, OSRAM optimizes the references of a deployed policy against a learned command--response model~\cite{osram}, and command adapters for frozen locomotion policies have been audited in simulation~\cite{li2026audit}. Keeping such optimization inside the model's data is a recognized concern in model-based optimization~\cite{mins,lagrassa2024,osram}.

\textbf{Frozen humanoid controllers.} TTCO optimizes the command of a frozen humanoid controller by sampling closed-loop simulations of the controller and robot~\cite{ttco}, and EgoHumanoid-V2 refines training labels offline with a learned local response model of a frozen tracker and fresh rollouts~\cite{egohumanoidv2}. Other work scores candidate motions by their executability under a frozen tracker~\cite{s3,texedo}\TODO{cite SAW, arXiv 2409.10308, after reading it}, adds reference or action residuals for compliance and terrain~\cite{ila,graft}, or learns a tracker's dynamics to fine-tune it~\cite{humanoidndm}. For humanoid grasping, HERO corrects the commanded grasp with measured end-effector feedback~\cite{hero}. SelfAim leaves the controller's weights untouched, needs neither a simulator nor a hand measurement at run time, and aims each reach once from the body state at the command.

\section{Problem Statement and Hypotheses}\label{sec:hyp}
% ===========================================================================

\textbf{Setting.} A frozen controller $\pi$ receives, at decision time $t$, a reach command $c$ (a hand target that our whole-body IK turns into a reference for $\pi$) and executes it from body state $s_t$. After the reach settles the right hand is at $p=\Phi_\pi(s_t,c)$; $\Phi_\pi$ is unknown and includes the controller, its command interface and the simulator. Given a target $p^\star$, \emph{direct execution} (\Ad) sends $c=p^\star$. SelfAim learns $f_\theta\approx\Phi_\pi$ and sends
\begin{equation}
c^\star=\arg\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\;\lVert f_\theta(s_t,c)-p^\star\rVert ,
\label{eq:inv}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is restricted to commands that are kinematically reachable from $s_t$ (\Rr, the default) or unconstrained (\Sinv).

\textbf{Hypotheses.} For each we state what would falsify it and what we learn either way.

\emph{H1 (aim).} Inverting $f_\theta$ brings the settled hand closer to the target than \Ad\ for every controller, across target heights, distances and body postures, with no trials at the test condition. \emph{Falsified if} paired hand errors under \Rr\ are not lower than under \Ad, or if the reduction holds only in a narrow band of geometry. \emph{Either way:} whether a frozen WBC's placement error is a learnable function of state and command.

\emph{H2 (success).} Aiming raises grasp success over \Ad\ for every controller without task data, by an amount that depends on the task. \emph{Falsified if} \Rr\ does not raise success over \Ad\ on a controller's pre-registered task set. \emph{Either way:} whether a better-aimed hand is worth building into manipulation stacks on frozen controllers.
% H2 history: 11-26 17:10 "gains concentrate where placement misses dominate" not supported (lmlcmp3, status §1.3b); 11-26 rewrite: "accuracy only partly turns into success" replaced by the positive form above after the GT-mask main table (status §1.3c). Confirmed by the user 11-26.

\emph{H3 (support).} The inversion is only as good as the model's coverage: unconstrained search (\Sinv) drives $c^\star$ outside the training distribution, where $f_\theta$ is wrong and the hand overshoots; restricting $\mathcal{C}$ to reachable commands avoids this. \emph{Falsified if} \Sinv's large errors are not concentrated on out-of-support commands.
% H3 falsifier shortened 11-26 (no S arm in the GT-mask run); confirmed by the user 11-26.

\emph{H4 (deployment).} Moving SelfAim to a new controller or simulator costs one data collection with the same recipe: a model trained on data from the target setting passes the accuracy gate on real task episodes. \emph{Falsified if} a re-collected model fails the gate on most tasks of the target benchmark. \emph{Either way:} how much a new deployment costs.

\textbf{Robot, controllers and commands.} We use a Unitree G1 with Dex3 hands (29 body joints) and three frozen controllers: ScaleBFM~\cite{scalebfm}, SONIC~\cite{sonic} and HEFT~\cite{heft}. A reach command $c$ consists of a hand target point, a 6-D wrist orientation, a stand/squat flag and a squat pelvis height. At the moment of the command, our whole-body IK plans a reference from the \emph{measured} state, and the controller tracks that reference through its native reference interface (e.g.\ body keypoints for ScaleBFM, an encoder over future reference frames for SONIC).
The hand point is 13\,cm ahead of the wrist; a reach has \emph{settled} once pelvis and both hands move slower than 2\,cm/s for 0.5\,s, with a 3\,s cap.

\textbf{Self-model.} $f_\theta$ maps the state at the command and the command to the settled hand point and wrist orientation, both expressed in the measured pelvis-heading frame at the command time. The state contains three body-state snapshots (0.3, 0.2 and 0.1\,s before the command), the current hand pose, and a summary of the IK reference: its final joint configuration, the change from the current one, the planned pelvis pose and the planned duration. Two auxiliary heads predict fall and settle probabilities. The model is an ensemble of five MLPs ($4\times512$, SiLU) trained on bootstrap resamples. Each member predicts a residual on the command, and its last layer is zero-initialised, so an untrained model reproduces direct execution. The loss is a Huber loss (2\,cm) on position, a squared loss on the 6-D rotation, and cross-entropy on the auxiliary heads; we train with AdamW for 30k steps and select the checkpoint by validation median error.

\textbf{Data.} All data come from the frozen controller in the target simulator, and none from task episodes. (i)~30k resets with two reaches each. About a third of the episodes start with a walk, and the rest start standing or squatting. Every reach is planned from the measured state once the previous one has settled. Targets are sampled inside an IK-probed reachable region, 0.05--1.5\,m high. (ii)~15k resets with four short reaches each, of the kind a grasp makes: the hand settles 5--12\,cm in front of the target and then enters slowly; adding them reduces the entry-phase error by 24--32\%. (iii)~Mid-trajectory samples: states every 0.2\,s along a reach become additional starting points, labeled with the same final landing, which lowers the dataset error by a further 14--26\%. Data are split by reset. Collecting the data takes \TODO{simulation time per controller} and training takes \TODO{GPU time}; the controller itself is never updated.
% src: status §1.5 (B51, B55): (ii) entry-phase error -24-32 %, (iii) dataset error -14-26 %. Cost numbers: ask 5090 in the batch update (pending_updates.md).

\textbf{Inversion.} Only the position target is changed; the wrist orientation is commanded as is. Starting at $c_0=p^\star$, the inversion iterates $c_{k+1}=\mathrm{clip}\big(c_k+p^\star-f_\theta(s_t,c_k)\big)$ six times, re-planning the reference at each step, and keeps the candidate minimizing $\lVert f_\theta-p^\star\rVert^2+\lambda\,\sigma^2$, where $\sigma$ is the ensemble spread and $\lambda=1$.\dnote{lambda only in code (lml\_tasks.py:82), not in entries}
\Rr\ checks each candidate's IK error. If it exceeds 2\,cm, \Rr\ bisects the candidate back toward $p^\star$ (six halvings) until the error is within 2\,cm; \Sinv\ skips this check. In grasping, the pre-grasp and the entry segment are each commanded once, with no re-correction after observing the result; the model is used for free-hand reaches before contact.

\textbf{Benchmarks and protocol.} LocoManiLab~\cite{locomanilab} provides seven grasping tasks (candle, apple, bowl, salt shaker, statue in a cabinet, dumbbell, watering can). In each episode the robot looks once from its start pose, walks to a stance planned from that view, and makes one corrective walk if leg odometry shows it is still at least 10\,cm off. We run 90 jittered episodes per task. Every episode is run once per arm on the same machine with the same perception\footnote{In LocoManiLab, object segmentation uses the simulator's ground-truth object mask; depth, point clouds, grasp planning and decisions are computed as usual.} and grasp choice, and arms are compared paired by episode. Besides \Ad\ and \Rr, two arms follow a collision-free arm route planned with cuRobo~\cite{curobo}, executed directly (\Croute) or with \Rr\ (\CRr). Success means the object is lifted by 30\,cm (10\,cm for the statue) and held for 1\,s. Success is tested with exact McNemar tests and hand error with sign tests. A self-model is used directly in a new setting only if its prediction error on real task episodes is at most 1.3 times its own validation error, with a 95\% CI upper bound of at most 1.5. SIMPLE~\cite{simple} and HumanoidArena~\cite{humanoidarena} (Isaac Lab~\cite{isaaclab}) complete the evaluation (Sec.~\ref{sec:plan}).

\section{Preliminary Results}\label{sec:prelim}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{H3: the inversion must stay inside the training support}
For ScaleBFM, the model's landing error is 0.7--1.7\,cm on IK-reachable commands but about 20\,cm on commands beyond the reachable boundary (IK error $>3$\,cm), which make up only 2--4\% of the training data. \subsection{H4: a new simulator costs one data collection}\label{sec:h4}
A model does not carry over between simulators unchanged: trained in LocoManiLab, it predicts the hand in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena with 1.06 to 6.62 times its own validation error, above the gate for five of the six pairs of controller and simulator. Re-collecting the data in the target simulator with the same recipe restores accuracy. We re-collected ScaleBFM's data in SIMPLE and evaluated the new model on real grasping episodes of our SIMPLE benchmark under direct execution (four tasks, 40 distinct layouts). On XMovePick, XMoveBendPick and BendPick, the median prediction error of the settled hand point is 0.36, 0.40 and 1.07\,cm, and the model passes the gate. On TabletopGraspMP (2.26\,cm; ratio 1.20, 95\% CI $[1.04,16.0]$), the table edge blocks the hand during the pre-grasp in all 12 failed direct-execution episodes, a contact that free-space data never contain and that the model misses by 28--40\,cm.

\section{Discussion}
% ===========================================================================
SelfAim divides the work of loco-manipulation on a frozen WBC in two: the controller keeps its balance and gait, and a learned model of where it puts the hand corrects every reach command. Our preliminary results indicate that this division already pays off in grasp success for three controllers with different command interfaces, at the cost of one data collection per controller and simulator. The model is trustworthy where its data are, which is why the inversion stays inside the reachable set. If E1 and E2 confirm H1 and H2 in SIMPLE and HumanoidArena, aiming becomes a reusable step for any manipulation stack built on released WBC checkpoints.
